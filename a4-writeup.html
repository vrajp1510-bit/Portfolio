Part 1

Q1

Looking at the Size portfolios, you can see a clear pattern: Size1 (1.16%) earns more than Size5 (0.91%). Smaller companies deliver higher monthly returns. This is called the size premium — investors get rewarded for taking on the extra risk of holding small, less stable companies.

For the B/M portfolios, BM5 (1.24%) earns significantly more than BM1 (0.94%). Value stocks (high B/M = cheap relative to book value) outperform growth stocks (low B/M = expensive). This is the value premium — companies that look cheap on paper tend to be financially stressed or out-of-favour, so the market compensates investors with higher returns.

Q2

Size1 STD = 0.0640 vs. Size5 STD = 0.0429 — small companies are roughly 50% more volatile than large ones. More risk, more return. Consistent.

BM5 STD = 0.0548 vs. BM1 STD = 0.0483 — value stocks are more volatile than growth stocks, which partly explains their higher returns.

For B/M and E/P portfolios, high-ratio portfolios tend to have somewhat higher volatility and higher returns, although the relation is less smooth than for size.

Q3

In an ideal case where CAPM works perfectly, then all alphas should be equal to zero since CAPM theory is based on the assumption that the only factor affecting returns is beta. In such a scenario, once you take into consideration the effects of market risk, there should be nothing extra to consider, and thus alpha is equal to zero. Nevertheless, in this case, alphas are not zero. Alphas for small stocks portfolios (Size1) and value portfolio (BM5) are positive, whereas alphas of portfolios of large stocks (Size5) and growth stocks (BM1) are almost zero or negative. Therefore, this indicates that CAPM does not give an entirely satisfactory explanation of returns from the stocks. Moreover, the scatter diagram between beta and average returns is positively related, meaning that portfolios with high betas earn high returns.

Q4

The long-short portfolio (BM5 − BM1) generates a positive average monthly return of 0.30%, confirming the value premium; however, this is not consistent across all periods during the 1998–2000 dot-com boom the strategy lost −0.17% per month as growth stocks dramatically outperformed value stocks.

The strategy sharply reversed during 2001–2004, generating 1.11% per month as the dot-com bubble burst and overvalued growth stocks collapsed, demonstrating that the value premium is cyclical and requires significant patience to capture, as extended periods of underperformance can persist before the premium is realized.

The return on value stocks over the past 10 years is 1.08, which is lower than their long-term average return of 1.24. During the COVID-19 period, value stock performance declined even further to around 1.00, indicating a significant underperformance relative to historical levels.

Value stocks underperformed over the past 10 years and during COVID primarily because interest rates were near zero, meaning the risk-free rate offered almost no return, which drove investors toward high-growth technology stocks seeking higher returns, reducing the demand for traditional value stocks and causing the value premium to diminish.
